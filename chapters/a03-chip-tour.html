The question

On the Wormhole spec page, the chip has 80 Tensix cores. Buy the single-chip card, the n150, and you get 72. Buy the dual-chip n300 and each chip gives you 64. The 32-chip Galaxy server gives you all 80 on every chip. It is the same silicon in every case. So:

Why does an 80-core chip sell as 72 or 64?

You can't answer that without a map of the chip, so this chapter draws one. We use the real coordinates from the SoC descriptor files that tt-metal ships, not a marketing diagram. Once you can read that map, three other questions get easy too: what the non-compute tiles are for, why a program sees a tidy 8×8 grid on a chip that physically has holes in it, and why the simulator calls one core (18,18) while the descriptor calls it (1,1).

Chapter A02 described a Tenstorrent chip as "a grid of independent cores with software-managed SRAM". Here we make that sentence concrete. What goes on inside one core waits until A04, and how the network between cores works waits until A08.

The chip as a city grid

Picture a city laid out as a perfect grid of blocks. Every block sits on a road intersection. Most blocks are factories, the places where the work happens. Down two avenues stand warehouses holding the raw materials. Along two streets are the docks, where shipments leave for other cities. One block is the airport, the link to the outside world, and one is city hall, which keeps the lights on.

Swap in the real names and you have a Wormhole die. Factories are Tensix tiles, warehouses are DRAM tiles, docks are Ethernet tiles, the airport is the PCIe tile to the host, and city hall is the ARC tile. The roads are the NoC (network-on-chip). Every tile has a NoC router, and that router is how the tile talks to any other tile. The ISA documentation says the NoCs "connect the various tiles to form a 2D torus", which it notes "is usually visualized as a 2D grid" (tt-isa-documentation/WormholeB0/README.md).

Step through the build below. Every square comes from tt_metal/soc_descriptors/wormhole_b0_80_arch.yaml; nothing is drawn by hand.

The final count is 80 + 18 + 16 + 1 + 1 + 4 = 120 = 10 × 12. Every grid position is accounted for. The four "router-only" positions are intersections with no building on them: a router that forwards traffic, but nothing a program can address.

Where the analogy breaks: in a real city, trucks from a factory can drive straight to any warehouse. Here every tile reaches every other tile only by hopping router to router, and each hop takes cycles. A DRAM tile also isn't a passive shelf. It answers read and write requests that arrive over the NoC. The ISA docs say its NIUs "respond to requests from other tiles; they are not capable of emitting requests of their own" (WormholeB0/DRAMTile/README.md). Warehouses only answer; factories place the orders.

On a GPU, the die layout is hidden behind the programming model. You launch thread blocks and the hardware scheduler picks an SM, and you never learn that SM 37 sits next to a memory controller. On Tenstorrent, the layout is part of the programming model. Kernels name the cores they talk to by coordinate, and how far apart two cores are on the grid affects how long their messages take.

Meet the tiles

Here is each tile type in turn, with the numbers that are worth remembering. Every number below is checked against the ISA documentation (Wormhole B0 / Blackhole A0) or the tt-metal descriptors.

Tensix: the factory

A Tensix tile has a block of SRAM called L1, five small "baby" RISC-V cores, a matrix engine and a vector engine, and two connections to the NoC. On Wormhole, L1 is 1464 KiB per tile. That is the worker_l1_size: 1499136 in the descriptor and TENSIX_SRAM_SIZE = 1464*1024 in the ISA spec. On Blackhole it is 1536 KiB. The ISA docs point out that the name is misleading: "This RAM is called 'L1', but this is somewhat of a misnomer, as it is suggestive of a hardware-managed cache, which is not the case - this is plain RAM" (WormholeB0/TensixTile/L1.md:3). Marketing pages round this to "1.5 MB per Tensix core". 1,499,136 bytes is indeed about 1.5 million bytes, but it is 1.43 MiB.

None of the five RISC-V cores does the heavy math. They issue instructions to the engines. In the ISA's words, "The RISCV cores are not intended to achieve high performance on their own; they are intended to oversee the other components that actually drive performance" (WormholeB0/TensixTile/README.md:16). A04 opens up this tile.

DRAM: the warehouse

Wormhole has 18 DRAM tiles that together expose 12 GiB of GDDR6. They come in six groups of three, and "each 2 GiB is exposed identically on 3 tiles" (WormholeB0/README.md). The descriptor encodes this as six lists of three coordinates with dram_bank_size: 2147483648. Why put the same memory behind three NoC addresses? Three entry points spread the traffic, so all the requests don't pile up on one router. Inside each group, the ISA's DRAM page describes "12x 1 GiB channels of GDDR6", which is two channels per group. That is why tt-metal's dram_views split each 2 GiB bank into two 1 GiB views. Blackhole has 24 DRAM tiles in eight groups of three, each group holding 4 GiB, for 32 GiB in total.

Ethernet: the docks

Wormhole has 16 Ethernet tiles, "each one with bidirectional 100 GbE connectivity". Each one also has its own baby RISC-V core (one on Wormhole, two on Blackhole) and its own L1: eth_l1_size: 262144 (256 KiB) on Wormhole and 524288 (512 KiB) on Blackhole. These tiles are how chips talk to each other. On products where some links are not wired to a connector, the ISA notes that the tiles "can still be used (for their RISCV and their L1), but they'll never receive any ethernet packets". Multi-chip systems are covered in A21.

PCIe: the airport

This tile links the chip to the host: PCI Express 4.0 x16 on Wormhole and 5.0 x16 on Blackhole. Wormhole has one PCIe tile. On an n300, the second chip's PCIe tile isn't connected to anything; "an ASIC without PCIe connected is still reachable via ethernet". Blackhole has two PCIe tiles, but "One of these tiles will be in use; the other will be permanently idle (at least in current products)".

ARC: city hall

The ARC tile holds "a four-core ARC CPU for executing firmware". It handles clocks, telemetry, resets and firmware updates. "Customers can mostly ignore this tile, as it does not execute customer workloads" (WormholeB0/ARCTile/README.md). Note that ARC is a different CPU architecture, not RISC-V. Blackhole adds a separate Security tile beside it.

L2CPU (Blackhole only): the office tower

Blackhole adds four L2CPU tiles. Each one contains "a coherent cluster of four SiFive x280 CPUs (for an aggregate total of sixteen CPU cores)", with private L1 and L2 caches per core and a shared 2 MiB L3 per cluster (BlackholeA0/README.md). These are real 64-bit Linux-capable cores, very different from the baby RISC-Vs. One oddity to keep in mind: tt-metal's SoC descriptor lists their grid positions as router_only, because Metalium never schedules kernels onto them.

Router-only: empty intersections

These positions have a NoC router but no endpoint that software uses. Wormhole has four of them, all in column 0. In Blackhole's descriptor, all of row 0 apart from DRAM, PCIe and ARC is router-only, and so is all of column 8 below ARC. As noted above, that column actually holds the L2CPU and Security tiles; the descriptor just doesn't expose them.

A Tenstorrent chip is a 2D grid of tiles on a NoC. Most tiles compute (Tensix), and the rest are memory (DRAM), chip-to-chip links (Ethernet), the host link (PCIe) and management (ARC, plus L2CPU and Security on Blackhole). Programs address tiles by grid coordinate.

Harvesting: selling imperfect chips

Back to the puzzle. A big die has many square millimetres of silicon, and every square millimetre has some chance of a manufacturing defect. If you only sold dies with zero defects, you would throw away a lot of them. The standard industry fix is harvesting: build in redundancy, test each die, and fuse off the broken part.

Wormhole harvests whole rows of Tensix tiles. The ISA documentation is explicit: "n150 boards have one fused-off row, and n300 boards have two fused-off rows per ASIC, and if any rows are fused off, then the particular fused-off rows will vary from ASIC to ASIC" (WormholeB0/NoC/Coordinates.md:44). With 8 Tensix tiles per row, the arithmetic falls out:

  • 80 − 0 × 8 = 80 (Galaxy modules)
  • 80 − 1 × 8 = 72 (n150)
  • 80 − 2 × 8 = 64 per chip (n300)

Firmware stops a fused-off tile by setting a "tile clock disable" bit in its NIU configuration: "firmware will set this to true for fused-off tiles" (WormholeB0/NoC/MemoryMap.md:226). It also tells every NIU to leave that row out of broadcasts. tt-metal enforces the limit when it picks a core layout: more than two harvested rows or columns throws "At most two rows or cols can be harvested" (tt_metal/llrt/core_descriptor.cpp:145-148).

Why throw away a whole row when only one tile is bad? Partly because the remaining tiles still have to form a clean rectangle. NoC multicast targets rectangles of tiles (see A08), and the coordinate remapping described below is set up per row and per column. A grid with one tile missing from the middle would break both. That reasoning is ours; the docs state the policy, not the motive.

The figure below is a toy model meant to build intuition. It is not Tenstorrent yield data, which is not public. Assume each Tensix row independently has some probability of containing a fatal defect, then sort the dies into bins.

Drag the slider. Even at modest defect rates, a large share of dies have exactly one or two bad rows. Without harvesting, those dies are scrap. With harvesting, they become n150s and n300s. A die with one bad row can also be sold as a 64-core part by fusing off one extra, healthy row. Binning is a business decision as much as a technical one.

You cannot tell which row is harvested from the product name. Two n150 cards can have different rows fused off. Code that hard-codes physical coordinates of Tensix tiles will work on one card and break on the next. That is the reason the coordinate systems in the next sections exist.

Three ways to name one core

Take the first Tensix tile, top left. It has (at least) three names, and each one exists for a reason.

1. Physical, or raw NoC coordinates

This is where the tile actually sits. NoC 0 puts (0,0) at the top-left tile and counts "in the direction of data flow (rightwards and downwards)". NoC 1 runs the other way, so its (0,0) is the bottom-right tile (Coordinates.md:7,13). The same tile therefore has two raw addresses. In ttsim's code, the NoC 1 coordinate is (9−x, 11−y) on Wormhole and (16−x, 11−y) on Blackhole. The descriptor YAMLs use NoC 0 coordinates, and UMD calls this system CoordSystem::NOC0.

tt-metal/tt_metal/soc_descriptors/wormhole_b0_80_arch.yaml:104-122 @7aad8aac

      9-0, 1-0, 8-0, 2-0, 7-0, 3-0, 6-0, 4-0,
      9-6, 1-6, 8-6, 2-6, 7-6, 3-6, 6-6, 4-6,
  ]

functional_workers:
  [
   1-1,   2-1,   3-1,   4-1,   6-1,   7-1,   8-1,   9-1,
   1-2,   2-2,   3-2,   4-2,   6-2,   7-2,   8-2,   9-2,
   ...
   1-11,  2-11,  3-11,  4-11,  6-11,  7-11,  8-11,  9-11,
  ]

Each entry is x-y. The worker list skips column 5 (DRAM) and rows 0 and 6 (Ethernet), so physical Tensix coordinates already have holes before any harvesting. The Ethernet list order defines the Ethernet IDs: E0 is at (9,0), E1 at (1,0), and so on. The IDs alternate from the outside in.

2. Translated coordinates

The NIU in every tile can rewrite coordinates on the way out. The ISA spec explains that firmware configures this "with the following aims in mind: Uniform coordinate system for both NoC #0 and NoC #1; Similar types of tile having a contiguous coordinate range; On products where one or two rows of Tensix tiles have been harvested… renumber rows so that harvested rows are always at the end; Still allow software to use raw NoC coordinates if it wants to" (Coordinates.md:19-23).

On Wormhole, X and Y are translated separately, using numbers above the raw range:

TranslatedMeans (NoC 0)
0–15Identity. Raw coordinates still work.
X = 16, 17Physical column 0 (PCIe, ARC, DRAM D0/D1) and column 5 (DRAM D2–D5)
X = 18–25The 8 Tensix columns 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9
Y = 16, 17The Ethernet rows 0 and 6
Y = 18…Good Tensix rows in top-to-bottom order, then harvested rows last, up to Y = 27

The ISA's worked example assumes physical rows 7 and 10 are fused off. Translated rows 18–25 then map to physical 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9, 11, and translated 26 and 27 map to the harvested rows 7 and 10 (Coordinates.md:46-63). The ISA example preset in the map reproduces exactly that table. ttsim configures the same tables when it boots a simulated chip:

ttsim/src/tile.cpp:157-168 @b3620244
        uint32_t translated_row = 0;
        // Firmware assigns logical rows to active physical rows first, then appends harvested rows.
        for (uint32_t harvested = 0; harvested <= 1; harvested++) {
            for (uint32_t physical_row = 0; physical_row < std::size(TENSIX_ROWS); physical_row++) {
                if (((logical_mask >> physical_row) & 1) == harvested) {
                    uint32_t physical_y = TENSIX_ROWS[physical_row];
                    noc_translation_table_set(
                        p_tile->noc_translation_y[noc], 18 + translated_row, noc ? (11 - physical_y) : physical_y);
                    translated_row++;
                }
            }
        }

Read it as two passes over TENSIX_ROWS = {1,2,3,4,5,7,8,9,10,11} (tile.cpp:63). The first pass (harvested == 0) gives the good rows translated slots 18, 19, 20 and so on. The second pass appends the harvested rows. noc ? (11 - physical_y) : physical_y stores the NoC 1 version of the same row in NoC 1's table, so translated coordinates mean the same thing on both NoCs. That's the "uniform coordinate system" aim from the spec.

3. Logical coordinates

TT-Metalium goes one step further. A logical worker coordinate counts only the usable Tensix tiles, starting at (0,0). On an n150 it runs from (0,0) to (7,8), with no holes and no knowledge of which physical row was lost. Host code uses logical coordinates to place kernels, and the runtime converts them to translated coordinates for the device. The core descriptor YAMLs say so in their first line: "Anything using [[#, #]] is logical coordinates" (tt_metal/core_descriptors/wormhole_b0_80_arch.yaml:1).

To check this on the simulator, I wrote a short host program (about 20 lines), gridinfo.cpp, that opens one device and prints the grid sizes and worker_core_from_logical_core() for a few cores. Here is the real output from this orb, with UMD log lines trimmed:

$ source sim-labs/workbench/env.sh wh; ./gridinfo (ttsim WH)
grid_size            = 10x12
logical_grid_size    = 8x9
compute grid         = 8x8
dram_grid_size       = 12x1
logical (0,0) -> virtual/translated (18,18)
logical (1,0) -> virtual/translated (19,18)
logical (0,1) -> virtual/translated (18,19)
logical (7,8) -> virtual/translated (25,26)

Several facts are visible at once. The full grid is 10×12. Only 8×9 = 72 workers are logical, so the simulated chip behaves like an n150 with one row harvested. Logical (0,0) is translated (18,18), which matches the table above. The last logical row, 8, lands on translated row 26, so translated row 27 is the harvested one. The "compute grid" is 8×8 because tt-metal reserves the last logical row for dispatch cores: dispatch_cores: [[0, -1], [1, -1], ...], where −1 means "last row" in the core descriptor. And dram_grid_size = 12 is the twelve 1 GiB views.

The mask's bits index TENSIX_ROWS. 0x40 is bit 6, and TENSIX_ROWS[6] is physical row y = 8. The ttsim n150 preset in the map uses exactly this. The n300 masks 0x41 and 0x05 harvest two rows per chip, and the 32-chip Galaxy case harvests nothing, which matches the 80/72/64 arithmetic above.

tt-metal makes the same link from the other direction. It turns "how many rows are harvested" into a product name, then loads that product's core layout:

tt-metal/tt_metal/llrt/core_descriptor.hpp:22-29 @7aad8aac
inline const std::string& get_product_name(tt::ARCH arch, uint32_t num_harvested_on_axis) {
    const static std::map<tt::ARCH, std::map<uint32_t, std::string>> product_name = {
        {tt::ARCH::WORMHOLE_B0, {{0, "galaxy"}, {1, "nebula_x1"}, {2, "nebula_x2"}}},
        {tt::ARCH::BLACKHOLE, {{0, "unharvested"}, {1, "1xharvested"}, {2, "2xharvested"}}},
        {tt::ARCH::QUASAR, {{0, "unharvested"}}}};

    return product_name.at(arch).at(num_harvested_on_axis);
}

"nebula_x1" is the internal name used for the n150 layout (one harvested row) and "nebula_x2" for the n300 layout (two). Note the axis in the variable name, num_harvested_on_axis. On Wormhole the axis is rows. On Blackhole it is columns.

tt-metal APIs call translated coordinates "virtual" (see the output above: virtual/translated), UMD calls them TRANSLATED, and the ISA calls the feature "coordinate translation". These are three names for one idea. Also, the "logical" coordinate in Metalium is not the same as the ISA's internal noc_id_logical register, which ttsim fills with the translated value.

What changes on Blackhole

Switch the map to Blackhole. The grid grows to 17×12 = 204 positions, and the layout changes:

  • Tensix columns x = 1–7 and 10–16 and rows y = 2–11: 14 × 10 = 140 tiles.
  • DRAM fills columns 0 and 9 from top to bottom. That's 24 tiles in 8 banks of 3. Each bank's three tiles are scattered around the column, for example bank 1 at rows 2, 10 and 3.
  • Ethernet takes most of row 1 (14 tiles). PCIe (2 tiles), ARC and many router-only positions make up row 0.
  • Column 8 holds ARC at the top, then Security and the four L2CPU clusters further down.

The biggest change for programmers is that Blackhole harvests columns, not rows. The ISA says: "If any Tensix columns are fused off (as two columns are on p100 boards), then X is remapped to put the fused columns at maximal X" (BlackholeA0/NoC/Coordinates.md:54). Blackhole translation is also combined rather than split per axis. The Tensix rows keep their raw Y. Only Tensix X is remapped, and the gap at X = 8, 9 stays: "X = 8 always refers to the column containing CPUs, and X = 9 always refers to a column containing DRAM". The other tile types move into high, otherwise unused ranges:

Translated (X, Y)Contents (from BlackholeA0/NoC/Coordinates.md:28-46)
(1–7, 10–16; 2–11)Tensix. On a 120-core part, the fused columns are pushed to X = 15, 16.
(17–18, 12–23)DRAM, three translated rows per 4 GiB bank
(19, 24)Whichever PCIe tile is connected to the host
(20–31, 25)The 12 usable Ethernet tiles
(8, 26–29) and (8, 30)The four L2CPU tiles and the Security tile

So the simulated Blackhole has 12 × 10 = 120 workers, not 140. ttsim's single-chip mask is P150[] = {0xC0} (tile.cpp:41). Bits 6 and 7 index TENSIX_COLUMNS, giving physical columns 7 and 10, the two next to the centre spine. The last logical column, 11, lands on translated X = 14, so X = 15 and 16 are where the harvested columns went, just as the ISA table says. The compute grid is 11×10 because one column is reserved for dispatch (the "2xharvested" layout in tt_metal/core_descriptors/blackhole_140_arch.yaml ends at [10, 9]). Choose the ttsim p150 preset in the map to see this layout.

How many RISC-V cores?

Tenstorrent chips are often described as "RISC-V chips". Here is the count, built up from the tile inventory:

Source of coresWormhole (full die)Blackhole (full die)
Tensix tiles × 5 baby RV32 cores80 × 5 = 400140 × 5 = 700
Ethernet tiles × baby cores per tile16 × 1 = 1614 × 2 = 28
SiFive x280 (64-bit) in L2CPU tiles04 × 4 = 16
Total (documented)416744
DRAM-tile RISC-V ("DRISC")none documentedyes, count unverified (see note)

Sources: 5 baby cores per Tensix tile and 1 or 2 per Ethernet tile come from the ISA's TensixTile/README.md and EthernetTile/README.md for each chip, mirrored in ttsim's RV32_CORES_PER_T_TILE 5 and RV32_CORES_PER_E_TILE 1 or 2 (src/config.h). The 16 x280s are in BlackholeA0/README.md. ARC's four cores are not RISC-V and are not counted.

Blackhole's DRAM tiles also contain small RISC-V cores. tt-metal has a memory map for them ("DRISC has 128KB L1", tt_metal/hw/inc/internal/tt-1xx/blackhole/dev_mem_map.h:323-327) and an optional "DRAM programmable cores" mode (tt_metal/impl/context/metal_env.cpp:111-120). The ISA docs have no Blackhole DRAM-tile page, so we could not confirm how many there are. Our research notes assume one per DRAM tile (24), which would make 768. Treat that as an upper estimate. On products the counts drop with harvesting: a 72-core n150 has 72 × 5 + 16 = 376 RISC-V cores enabled.

Counting "cores" across architectures is close to meaningless. An NVIDIA "CUDA core" is one FP32 lane. A baby RISC-V is a whole tiny in-order CPU that doesn't do the tensor math at all, and the matrix engine next to it does far more work per cycle than the RISC-V. Compare chips on FLOP/s, bytes per second and bytes of SRAM, not on core counts.

The conflicts, spelled out

  • p150 core count. The ISA README's product table gives p150 140 Tensix tiles and p100 120. docs.tenstorrent.com gives 120 for p100a, p150a and p150b alike. Two pieces of evidence lean towards 120 for current p150 cards. First, the docs' 664 TFLOPS (BLOCKFP8) matches 120 × 4096 × 1.35 GHz = 663.6, using the 4096 FLOP/cycle per core that A06 derives, while 140 cores would give 774. Second, ttsim's p150 model harvests two columns (mask 0xC0). The ISA table may describe the design intent or a different SKU. We can't settle this from public sources.
  • Wormhole FP8 peak. The n150's quoted FP8 figure is 262 TFLOPS, but 72 × 4096 × 1 GHz = 295. The BLOCKFP8 (148) and FP16 (74) figures do fit 295 ÷ 2 and ÷ 4. We have no source for the 0.89 factor. The Galaxy page's 9.3 PFLOPS for 32 chips (about 292 per chip) shows the same ratio against 80 × 4096 = 328, though the current page labels that figure Block FP8 rather than FP8.
  • Galaxy per-chip numbers. Earlier versions of the Galaxy page listed 14 GT/s memory and 16 × 200 Gbps Ethernet per chip. The current page (checked 2026-10-30) lists 384 GB at 9.2 TB/s, about 288 GB/s per chip, which fits 12 GT/s, and "up to 16× 100 GbE links per ASIC", which matches the ISA. Treat older per-chip figures you find quoted elsewhere with care.
  • Blackhole Ethernet. The die has 14 tiles, and the descriptor lists all 14. The ISA says 12 are usable, 8 are wired to the p150's four QSFP-DD ports, and each port is a pair of tiles running at 800G. ttsim marks E12 and E13 as harvested (ETH_LIVE_STATUS_MASK 0x0FFF).

What ttsim builds

The simulator hard-codes the same geometry. If you read ttsim/src, these are the constants that set up the map you just explored:

ttsim/src/sim.h:73-85 @b3620244
#if TT_ARCH_VERSION == 0
#define NUM_TILE_COLS 10
#define NUM_TILE_ROWS 12
#define NONTENSIX_COL_MASK 0x21
#define NONTENSIX_ROW_MASK 0x41
#elif TT_ARCH_VERSION == 1
#define NUM_TILE_COLS 17
#define NUM_TILE_ROWS 12
#define NONTENSIX_COL_MASK 0x301
#define NONTENSIX_ROW_MASK 0x03
#endif
#define TENSIX_COL_MASK (((1ul << NUM_TILE_COLS) - 1) & ~NONTENSIX_COL_MASK)
#define TENSIX_ROW_MASK (((1ul << NUM_TILE_ROWS) - 1) & ~NONTENSIX_ROW_MASK)

TT_ARCH_VERSION is 0 for Wormhole and 1 for Blackhole. 0x21 = bits 0 and 5, the two Wormhole DRAM columns. 0x41 = bits 0 and 6, the two Ethernet rows. On Blackhole, 0x301 = columns 0, 8 and 9 (DRAM, the CPU spine, DRAM) and 0x03 = rows 0 and 1. ttsim writes these masks, combined with any harvested rows or columns, into each NIU's ROUTER_CFG_1 and ROUTER_CFG_3 (tile.cpp:141-142). The ISA defines those registers as the "Broadcast opt-out mask for columns/rows" (WormholeB0/NoC/MemoryMap.md:238-253). So the simulator, like real firmware, makes sure a multicast never lands on a DRAM column, an Ethernet row, or a fused-off row.

src/config.h:13-16 and :50-53 give the tile counts, NUM_T_TILES 80 / 140 and NUM_E_TILES 16 / 14, and NUM_DRAM_CHANNELS 6 / 8. The comment on the Wormhole value says "technically it's 12 channels, 2 for each of the 6 DRAM tiles, but doesn't matter". The function coord_to_tile in tile.cpp:723 is the inverse map. Give it a raw NoC coordinate and it returns 'T', 'E', 'D', 'P' or 'A' plus an index. For Wormhole Tensix tiles it computes tile_x = x−1 (x ≤ 4) or x−2 (x ≥ 6), and tile_y = y−1 (y ≤ 5) or y−2 (y ≥ 7), which squeezes out the DRAM column and the Ethernet row. ttsim does not model the L2CPU tiles: #define ENABLED_L2CPU_MASK 0 // no L2 cpus modeled (tile.cpp:369).

Recap

  • A Tenstorrent die is a 2D grid of tiles on a NoC: Wormhole is 10×12 = 120 positions and Blackhole 17×12 = 204, both taken straight from tt-metal's SoC descriptors.
  • Tile types: Tensix (compute and L1), DRAM (each bank exposed on 3 tiles), Ethernet (chip-to-chip, with its own RISC-V and L1), PCIe (host), ARC (management firmware), plus L2CPU (16 x280s) and Security on Blackhole, and router-only gaps.
  • Harvesting fuses off defective Tensix rows (Wormhole) or columns (Blackhole). That is why 80 becomes 72 (n150) or 64 (n300), and 140 becomes 120.
  • Every core has physical NoC 0 and NoC 1 coordinates, a firmware-configured translated coordinate that pushes harvested rows or columns to the end, and a Metalium logical coordinate counting only usable workers from (0,0).
  • On ttsim, Wormhole is modelled as an n150 (row y = 8 harvested, 8×9 logical, 8×8 compute) and Blackhole as a 120-core p150 (columns 7 and 10 harvested, 12×10 logical, 11×10 compute).
  • RISC-V count: 416 on a full Wormhole, 744 documented on a full Blackhole (more if the DRAM-tile cores are counted). None of them does the tensor math.

Check yourself

A Wormhole chip has rows y = 3 and y = 9 fused off. What is the translated Y of the Tensix tile at physical (6, 10)?

The good Tensix rows in order are 1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10, 11 (skipping 3 and 9; rows 0 and 6 are Ethernet). They take translated slots 18, 19, …, 25. Row 10 is the 7th good row, so it gets slot 18 + 6 = 24. Its X: physical column 6 is the 5th Tensix column (1, 2, 3, 4, 6, …), so translated X = 18 + 4 = 22, and logical (4, 6). Rows 3 and 9 get slots 26 and 27. You can check this in the map: select a Tensix tile in row 3 and press Harvest a row, then do the same for row 9, with labels set to translated.

Why does tt-metal show an 8×8 compute grid on an n150 when 72 Tensix cores are enabled?

72 = 8 × 9 logical workers. The n150 core descriptor ("nebula_x1") reserves the last logical row, [[0,-1] … [7,-1]], for dispatch cores that run the runtime's command-queue firmware. That leaves 8 × 8 = 64 for user compute. The ttsim run printed exactly this: logical_grid_size = 8x9, compute grid = 8x8.

A DRAM bank on Wormhole appears at three NoC coordinates. If you write to (0,0) and read the same address from (0,11), what do you get?

The same data. Both coordinates belong to bank D0, and the ISA says "each 2 GiB is exposed identically on 3 tiles". The three entry points exist so traffic from different parts of the grid can reach the bank through different routers. (Memory ordering across entry points is a separate question, covered in A08 and A09.)

Why can't Blackhole simply harvest rows, as Wormhole does?

The docs state the policy, not the motive. The geometry suggests a reason, though. Blackhole has 14 Tensix columns and only 10 Tensix rows. Removing a row costs 14 tiles (10%), while removing a column costs 10 (7%). Columns are also how its translation hardware remaps Tensix tiles, with X = 8 and 9 kept fixed. What the sources do confirm: p100 has two columns fused off, translated Tensix X is remapped so fused columns sit at maximal X, and tt-metal's Blackhole layouts are named by harvested-column count.

You hard-code physical NoC coordinate (9, 11) as the "last core" in a kernel. On which Wormhole products could this break?

Any harvested part: n150 or n300. If row 11 is the fused-off row on that particular chip, (9, 11) is a disabled tile. Which row is fused off varies from chip to chip, so your code might work on your card and fail on a colleague's. Use logical coordinates on the host and let the runtime translate them, or use the translated scheme, where harvested rows are always at the end.

How many baby RISC-V cores does an n300 card have enabled in total?

Per chip: 64 Tensix × 5 = 320, plus 16 Ethernet tiles × 1 = 16, giving 336. Two chips: 672. The Ethernet tiles count even when their link isn't connected, because the ISA says they "can still be used (for their RISCV and their L1)". ARC's cores are not RISC-V.
