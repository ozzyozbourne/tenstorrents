06. The matrix engine and math fidelity

By the end you will know where a matmul's operands sit inside a Tensix core, why one 32×32 tile product takes exactly 16 instructions, how 5-bit × 7-bit multipliers produce a full BF16 product in up to four passes, and how to work out a chip's peak TFLOPS with pencil and paper.

The question

A BF16 number carries 8 significant bits: a hidden leading 1 followed by 7 stored mantissa bits. Multiplying two of them exactly means multiplying two 8-bit integers, which produces a 16-bit result built from 8 × 8 = 64 one-bit partial products. A matrix engine that multiplies thousands of pairs per cycle would need thousands of 8×8-bit multipliers.

The Tensix matrix engine does not have them. The ISA documentation is explicit: "The hardware multipliers consume at most 5 bits of SrcA magnitude/mantissa and at most 7 bits of SrcB magnitude/mantissa" (tt-isa-documentation/WormholeB0/TensixTile/TensixCoprocessor/MVMUL.md:3). Five bits times seven bits is 35 partial products, a little over half of 64.

So the question for this chapter is: how do 5×7-bit multipliers compute a BF16 matmul? The short answer is "the same way you multiply 47 × 38 in your head: split it into pieces and add them up". You choose how many of the pieces to compute. That choice is called math fidelity, and it is the main dial between speed and precision on this hardware. Before we get to the bits, though, we need to know where the numbers sit while they are being multiplied, and what one instruction actually does.

Where the operands live: SrcA, SrcB and Dst

In A04 you met the Tensix coprocessor: unpackers move data from L1 into registers, the FPU (the "Matrix Unit") does arithmetic, and packers move results back to L1. The FPU never touches L1 itself. It works only on three register files:

  • SrcA and SrcB, the two input registers. Each has 2 banks of 64 rows × 16 columns of 19-bit data (SrcASrcB.md:3; ttsim declares the same shape as src_a[2][SRC_ROWS][ROW_SIZE] with SRC_ROWS 64, ROW_SIZE 16 in src/sim.h:51-54,259-260). Unpacker 0 fills SrcA and unpacker 1 fills SrcB.
  • Dst, the accumulator. It is 1024 rows × 16 columns × 16 bits, which can also be viewed as 512 rows × 16 columns × 32 bits (Dst.md:3). It has no reset value (Dst.md:6).

Why 64 rows of 16? From A05: a 32×32 tile is stored as four 16×16 faces, and four faces stacked on top of each other are exactly 64 rows of 16. One Src bank holds one tile. The 19-bit width is just enough for TF32 (1 sign + 8 exponent + 10 mantissa). BF16 is stored in the same slot with the three lowest mantissa bits set to zero (SrcASrcB.md:56-57), which is why FP32 data loses precision the moment it is unpacked into a Src register.

Why two banks?

Two banks let the unpacker fill one bank while the FPU reads the other. Each bank has an owner, AllowedClient, which is either the unpackers or the Matrix Unit (SrcASrcB.md:5-14). When the FPU finishes with a bank it hands it back and flips to the other. This is ordinary double buffering, the same idea as the two-tile circular buffers you will meet in A11, but done in hardware for registers.

Dst capacity in tiles

A tile occupies 64 rows of Dst in 16-bit mode, so Dst holds 1024 / 64 = 16 tiles of BF16. In 32-bit mode there are 512 rows, so it holds 8 tiles of FP32. tt-metal usually runs Dst in "half-sync" mode, where the math thread fills one half while the packer drains the other (you will meet this handshake in A12). That halves the usable count again. The tech report gives the same numbers: "if using DstSync::Half, then Float16_b can fit 8 tiles, while Float32 can only fit 4" (tech_reports/matrix_engine/matrix_engine.md:83).

RegisterShapeDatumCapacity in 32×32 tiles
SrcA2 banks × 64 × 1619 bit (TF32, BF16, FP16, INT8)1 per bank
SrcB2 banks × 64 × 1619 bit1 per bank
Dst, 16-bit1024 × 16BF16, FP1616 (8 with half-sync)

Turning on fp32_dest_acc_en gives you an FP32 accumulator, but it costs half of Dst. Ops that keep several output tiles in Dst at once (matmul subblocks, which you will meet in A15) must shrink their subblocks to fit.

One instruction: MVMUL

Almost all of a matmul's arithmetic is done by one instruction, MVMUL. Its ISA summary reads: "An aligned 8x16 matrix from SrcB is multiplied with an aligned 16x16 matrix from SrcA, and the resultant 8x16 matrix added element-wise to an aligned 8x16 block of Dst" (MVMUL.md:3). In symbols:

Dst[8×16] += SrcB[8×16] @ SrcA[16×16]

Note the order: SrcB is on the left. Each of the 8 × 16 = 128 outputs is a 16-term dot product, so one MVMUL performs 8 × 16 × 16 = 2048 multiply-accumulates, or 4096 floating-point operations if you count the multiply and the add separately, as FLOP counts conventionally do. The FPU accepts one MVMUL per cycle, with a latency of 5 cycles (MatrixUnit.md:25).

Here is the core of the ISA's functional model, trimmed to the floating-point case:

tt-isa-documentation/WormholeB0/TensixTile/TensixCoprocessor/MVMUL.md:143-158 @be833101 (float branch only)
for (unsigned i = 0; i < NumRows; ++i) {      // NumRows = 8
  for (unsigned j = 0; j < 16; ++j) {
      float x = 0;
      for (unsigned k = 0; k < 16; ++k) {
        x += SrcBMatrix[i][k].f * SrcAMatrix[k][j].f;
      }
      Multiplied[i][j].f = x;
  }
}

It is a textbook matrix multiply. The interesting parts are what happens before this loop, where SrcBMatrix and SrcAMatrix are filled through functions called SrcBFidelityBits and SrcAFidelityBits (MVMUL.md:124,136), and after it, where the result is added into Dst. We'll come back to both.

Which matmul operand goes where? In tt-metal's matmul LLK, the left matrix (in0) is unpacked into SrcB and the right matrix (in1) into SrcA:

tt-metal/tt_metal/tt-llk/tt_llk_wormhole_b0/llk_lib/llk_math_matmul.h:343-344 @7aad8aac
    // in0 - loaded to SrcB
    // in1 - loaded to SrcA

That matches the ISA's SrcB @ SrcA: the left operand of the instruction is the left matrix of the matmul. Keep it in mind, because the two inputs are not treated symmetrically: SrcA gets the 5-bit side of the multiplier and SrcB the 7-bit side.

A tile product is 16 MVMULs

Now scale up to a full tile: C (32×32) = in0 (32×32) @ in1 (32×32). Split each tile into its 2×2 grid of 16×16 faces, as in A05. Block matrix multiplication works on faces exactly the way ordinary multiplication works on numbers:

Cij = in0i0 · in10j + in0i1 · in11j   for i, j ∈ {0, 1}

That is 4 output faces × 2 terms = 8 face-by-face products, each a 16×16 @ 16×16 multiply. An MVMUL takes only 8 rows of the left operand at a time, so each face product needs 2 MVMULs, one for the top 8 rows ("slab") and one for the bottom 8. In total that is 2 × 2 × 2 faces × 2 slabs = 16 MVMULs per tile. Check: 16 × 2048 = 32,768 = 32³ multiply-accumulates. ✓

Step through the order in which tt-metal actually issues them. Faces of in0 are labelled B0–B3 because they sit in SrcB; faces of in1 are A0–A3. Each face takes 16 rows of its Src bank, and each output face takes 16 rows of Dst.

Every number in that animation comes from the LLK's recorded instruction sequence. For the ordinary 32×32 × 32×32 case it records 16 MVMULs into the replay buffer (replay_buf_len is 16, llk_math_matmul.h:361-362); 15 of them are shown below, and the 16th, the bottom slab of B3·A3, is issued at line 440 (or at one of lines 448–463 in the LoFi branch) with an address mode that resets the row counters.

The instruction itself carries no row numbers. Instead, three row counters (RWCs) for SrcA, SrcB and Dst are advanced after each instruction by an "address mode" (ADDR_MOD_0 to _3). ADDR_MOD_0 means "SrcB += 8 rows, Dst += 8 rows": move to the next slab. The comments read as "what happens after this instruction", which is how the rows in the animation were derived. Then the sequence is wrapped in a loop whose count is the number of fidelity phases:

tt-metal/tt_metal/tt-llk/tt_llk_wormhole_b0/llk_lib/llk_math_matmul.h:469-470 @7aad8aac
constexpr std::uint32_t inner_loops = high_fidelity ? to_underlying(math_fidelity) : 1;
ckernel_template tmp(1 /* outer loop */, inner_loops, lltt::replay_insn(ckernel::math::replay_buf_offset, replay_buf_len));

So at HiFi4 the whole 16-instruction sequence runs 4 times. The last MVMUL of each pass uses an address mode that resets the row counters and increments the fidelity phase (ADDR_MOD_5, set at lines 80-88 with .fidelity = {.incr = fidelity_increment}). This gives the "16 / 32 / 48 / 64 cycles per tile" that the GEMM tech report uses for LoFi through HiFi4 (tech_reports/GEMM_FLOPS/GEMM_FLOPS.md:107).

What is verified and what is inferred. Verified in the source: the 16-instruction replay buffer, the face labels and counter updates in the LLK comments, the phase loop, and the MVMUL shape in the ISA and in ttsim (tensix_matmul_op checks that the Dst row is a multiple of 8 and the SrcB row a multiple of 8, src/tensix.cpp:1806-1808). Inferred: the exact Dst row for each step comes from reading the comments, not from tracing the RWC registers. I also have not checked whether the unpacker stores in1's faces transposed in SrcA. The "srca+=32 if transposed" remarks show that the face order changes in that mode. Mathematically, the picture is the block-matrix identity above, whatever the physical layout. Lab B07 dumps one real MVMUL's registers from an instrumented ttsim.
An NVIDIA tensor core is also driven by fixed-shape matrix instructions (mma) that a warp issues collectively, and a GEMM kernel is likewise a loop of them. The differences are in who sequences them and where the accumulator lives. On a GPU the accumulators are spread across the warp's ordinary registers, and the compiler schedules the loop. On Tensix the accumulator is a dedicated 16-tile register file (Dst), and a tiny in-order RISC-V pushes a pre-recorded instruction sequence through the replay buffer.

The multipliers are narrow

Back to the opening puzzle. Forget binary for a moment and do 47 × 38 in decimal, with a rule: your multiplier can only handle one digit times one digit. Long multiplication handles it fine:

47 × 38 = (40 + 7)(30 + 8) = 40·30 + 7·30 + 40·8 + 7·8 = 1200 + 210 + 320 + 56 = 1786

Four small products, each shifted by a power of ten, then added. Notice how unequal they are. The "big × big" term, 1200, is already 67% of the answer. If you were in a hurry and computed only 40·30, you'd be 33% off. Add 7·30 and 40·8 and you are within 3%. The "small × small" term, 7·8, matters least.

The matrix engine does exactly this in binary. A BF16 significand is 8 bits: 1.m₆m₅m₄m₃m₂m₁m₀. The multiplier takes a 5-bit piece of the SrcA operand and a 7-bit piece of the SrcB operand. So split SrcA's 8 bits into a high piece of 5 (the hidden 1 and m₆…m₃) and a low piece of 3 (m₂m₁m₀). Split SrcB's 8 bits into a high piece of 7 (the hidden 1 and m₆…m₁) and a low piece of 1 (m₀). That gives four products, each of which fits the multiplier:

PhaseSrcA pieceSrcB piecePartial-product bitsWeight
0high 5 (1 + m₆…m₃)high 7 (1 + m₆…m₁)5 × 7 = 35heaviest
1low 3 (m₂…m₀)high 73 × 7 = 21≈ 2⁻⁴ of phase 0
2high 5low 1 (m₀)5 × 1 = 5≈ 2⁻⁷ of phase 0
3low 3low 13 × 1 = 3≈ 2⁻¹¹ of phase 0

35 + 21 + 5 + 3 = 64: the four pieces tile the full 8×8 grid of partial products with nothing missing and nothing counted twice. A pass of the matrix engine that computes one of these pieces for every element is a fidelity phase. The names you'll see in tt-metal code count phases: LoFi runs phase 0 only, HiFi2 runs phases 0–1, HiFi3 phases 0–2, and HiFi4 all four.

Fidelity phases, bit by bit

The ISA's fidelity table for BF16 says the same thing in words (SrcASrcB.md:121-125): phase 0 uses the "implicit 1 bit and most significant four mantissa bits" of SrcA and the "implicit 1 bit and most significant six mantissa bits" of SrcB; phase 1 uses the "remaining three mantissa bits" of SrcA; phase 2 uses the "remaining mantissa bit" of SrcB; phase 3 uses both remainders. The ISA's pseudocode expresses the SrcA split as a mask on an FP32 value:

tt-isa-documentation/WormholeB0/TensixTile/TensixCoprocessor/MatrixUnit.md:143-153 @be833101
float SrcAFidelityBits(float x, uint2_t FidelityPhase) {
  union {uint32_t u; float f;} bits;
  bits.f = x;
  if ((FidelityPhase & 1) == 0) {
    bits.u &= 0xfff80000; // Sign, Exp, implicit 1 of Man, next four Man bits.
    return bits.f;
  } else {
    bits.u &= 0xfff83fff; // Isolate the next five Man bits not consumed by prior branch.
    return x - bits.f;
  }
}

Bit 0 of the phase number selects SrcA's piece, and bit 1 selects SrcB's piece (SrcBFidelityBits, lines 155-165, tests FidelityPhase & 2). So phase 1 = (A low, B high), phase 2 = (A high, B low), phase 3 = (A low, B low). The ISA warns that its floating-point model is "a rough guide rather than an exact description" (MVMUL.md:223). ttsim, which is bit-exact against silicon, does it on integers:

ttsim/src/tensix.cpp:1690-1707 @b3620244
uint32_t man_a = (a & 0x3FF) | 0x400; // 11 bits
uint32_t man_b = (b & 0x3FF) | 0x400;

int32_t exp_prod = exp_a + exp_b + exp_prod_adj;
if (fidelity_phase & 1) {
    man_a = (man_a >> 1) & 31; // 5 bits
} else {
    man_a >>= 6; // 5 bits
}
if (fidelity_phase & 2) {
    man_b = (man_b & 15) << 3; // 7 bits
} else {
    man_b >>= 4; // 7 bits
}

signs[i] = sign_a ^ sign_b;
exps[i] = exp_prod;
mans[i] = man_a * man_b; // up to 12 bits

Line by line:

  • man_a and man_b are 11-bit significands: the hidden 1 (0x400) and the 10-bit TF32 mantissa field from the 19-bit Src datum. A BF16 value fills only the top 7 of those 10 bits.
  • Even phases take man_a >> 6, the top 5 bits (hidden 1 and four mantissa bits). Odd phases take bits 5…1, the next five. For BF16 those five are m₂m₁m₀ followed by two zeros. Bit 0 is never used, which is the ISA's footnote "the least significant bit of the FP16 or TF32 mantissa is ignored" for SrcA (MVMUL.md:13).
  • For SrcB, phases 0 and 1 take man_b >> 4, the top 7 bits. Phases 2 and 3 take the low 4 bits, shifted left by 3 so they still fill a 7-bit multiplier input. For BF16 only one of those four bits (m₀) can be non-zero.
  • man_a * man_b is one 5-bit × 7-bit product, at most 12 bits. That is the multiplier.

Where do the shifts go? Into the exponent. When the low piece of A is used, the product is worth 2⁵ less than when the high piece is, so the exponent is lowered by 5. For B's low piece the adjustment is 7: a weight of 2⁴, plus the 3 bits of left shift that have to be undone:

ttsim/src/tensix.cpp:1572-1581 @b3620244
static inline int32_t fpu_exp_prod_adj(bool fp32_acc, uint32_t fidelity_phase, bool fp_exponent_8b) {
    int32_t exp_prod_adj = fp_exponent_8b ? -127 : (fp32_acc ? (127 - 30) : -15); // rebias exponent to desired range
    if (fidelity_phase & 1) {
        exp_prod_adj -= 5;
    }
    if (fidelity_phase & 2) {
        exp_prod_adj -= 7;
    }
    return exp_prod_adj;
}

Now see it happen. Type two numbers (they are rounded to BF16), or pick a preset. The grid is the full 8×8 table of one-bit partial products: column c is a bit of x's significand, row r a bit of y's, and a cell is 1 when both bits are 1. Each step lights one quadrant, the piece computed in that phase. The table on the right runs the actual ttsim arithmetic: the panel's JavaScript is a line-by-line port of mvmul, fpu_accum_normalize_encode, fpu_align_term and fpu_exp_prod_adj (src/tensix.cpp:1477-1581, 1669-1741), accumulating into a zeroed Dst one phase at a time.

Things to try in the calculator:

  • All ones (1.9921875 = 1.1111111₂) lights every cell, so every phase contributes. With an FP32 Dst the precision climbs from about 5 bits (LoFi) to about 8 (HiFi2), then 13, then exact (HiFi4).
  • π × e: BF16(e) = 2.71875 = 1.0101110₂ × 2¹, and its last mantissa bit is 0. Phases 2 and 3 multiply by that bit, so they add exactly nothing. HiFi2 is already exact for this pair.
  • Switch Dst to BF16. The product is rounded to 8 significant bits after every phase. Phase 3's piece is never more than 7/16384 of the product, far below half a BF16 ulp (2⁻⁹ of the value), so it is rounded away. HiFi3 and HiFi4 then give the same bits. The simulator run below shows this happening across a whole matmul.
A fidelity phase is one complete pass of the matrix engine over the data, with a different slice of each operand's significand. LoFi computes only the "big × big" piece; HiFi4 computes all four and covers the full 8×8-bit product. Cost scales linearly with the number of phases. Precision does not: each extra phase is worth far less than the one before.
Asymmetry matters. SrcA (your matmul's in1, usually the weights) loses 3 bits under LoFi, while SrcB (in0, usually the activations) loses only 1. A LoFi matmul is therefore much more accurate when the in1 operand already has few mantissa bits, for example weights stored as BFP8 or BFP4. That is exactly the "BFP8 weights + LoFi/HiFi2" pairing you will see in model configs.

Validating the calculator against ttsim itself

A calculator that claims to be bit-exact should be tested like code. I compiled ttsim's own functions, copied verbatim (src/tensix.cpp:1477-1581 and 1669-1741) into a small C++ driver, and compared it with the JavaScript above, extracted from this page between its BEGIN/END ttsim-port markers. The test used 30,000 random cases: single products and full 16-term dot products, positive and mixed signs, narrow and full exponent ranges, FP32 and BF16 Dst, four phases each. The result was 0 mismatches in the FP32 bit patterns. As a check that the test can fail at all, changing manA >> 6 to >> 5 in the port produced 22,786 mismatches. One branch, the Wormhole "renormalisation of −1" quirk (tensix.cpp:1533-1537), was never reached by the random inputs, so it is ported but not exercised.

How many phases each format needs

Once you see fidelity as "which pieces of the significand grid are computed", the ISA's table of how many phases each format needs becomes something you can derive rather than memorise.

Format in SrcSignificant bits per valueFits SrcA 5 bits?Fits SrcB 7 bits?Phases for full precision (ISA)
FP8 (e5m2), BFP4, BFP2≤ 3yesyesphase 0 only
BFP8 (BFP8_b)≤ 7no (needs 5 + 3)yesphases 0 and 1
BF168nono (needs 7 + 1)all four
TF32, FP1611no (5 + 5 leaves 1 bit)7 + 4not possible for SrcA

The last column is the ISA's (SrcASrcB.md:110-115); the middle columns are the reasoning. BFP8 is the instructive row. From A05, a BFP8 value stores a sign and 7 mantissa bits and shares one exponent with its block. The leading 1 is not hidden: it is stored explicitly among those 7 bits ("we also explicitly store the hidden bit of 1 for bfloat8, so we are really rounding to 6 bits total", tech_reports/data_formats/data_formats.md:12). So a BFP8 value has at most 7 significant bits, and when it is unpacked to BF16 they fit SrcB's 7-bit slice in one go. Only SrcA needs its second slice, so phases 0 and 1 are enough. For TF32 and FP16, SrcA's two slices cover 5 + 5 = 10 of the 11 significant bits, so the last bit is never multiplied in any phase (SrcASrcB.md:135).

The ISA also lists "reasonable" precision: phases 0 and 1 for BF16 (SrcASrcB.md:114). In the calculator, HiFi2 leaves out only SrcB's last mantissa bit, an error of at most 1/129 ≈ 0.8% per product, and about 0.25% on average for random data. That is why HiFi2 is a common default for BF16 matmuls.

In code you pick the fidelity per compute kernel through MathFidelity. Notice the enum values:

tt-metal/tt_metal/api/tt-metalium/base_types.hpp:13-19 @7aad8aac
enum class MathFidelity : uint8_t {
    LoFi = 0,
    HiFi2 = 2,
    HiFi3 = 3,
    HiFi4 = 4,
    Invalid = 0xff,
};

HiFi2/3/4 equal their phase counts, but LoFi is 0, not 1. The LLK treats anything other than LoFi as "high fidelity" (cmath_common.h:333-336) and uses the enum value as the loop count (to_underlying(math_fidelity) above). The same applies to element-wise multiplication (ELWMUL), which uses the same multipliers and the same phases (ELWMUL.md:3). Element-wise add does not multiply, so it ignores fidelity.

Accumulating in Dst

Each phase produces partial products that must be added, both across the 16 terms of a dot product and on top of what Dst already holds. Fidelity controls what is multiplied; Dst's format controls how much of the sum survives. ttsim's mvmul does the addition in three steps:

  1. The 16 products are split into two groups of 8. Each group is aligned to its largest exponent and rounded to an integer, then summed (tensix.cpp:1711-1739).
  2. The two group sums and the old Dst value are aligned to the largest of their three exponents. If Dst is not FP32, the low 13 bits are rounded away during this alignment:
ttsim/src/tensix.cpp:1505-1510 @b3620244
// Low 13 mantissa bits are rounded away unless fp32_acc, in the same step as the alignment: rounding at both
// would round a term just below half an ulp up
uint32_t lsb = fp32_acc ? 0 : 13;
man_sop0 = fpu_align_term(man_sop0, sign_sop0, exp - exp_sop0, lsb);
man_sop1 = fpu_align_term(man_sop1, sign_sop1, exp - exp_sop1, lsb);
man_dst = fpu_align_term(man_dst, 0, exp - exp_dst, lsb);
  1. The sum is renormalised and rounded to the Dst format: 24 significant bits for FP32, 8 for BF16 (tensix.cpp:1532-1569).

The consequence is subtle. Each term is rounded separately to Dst's grid before the add. With a BF16 Dst, a term worth less than half a BF16 ulp of the running sum rounds to zero, no matter how many such terms arrive. The ISA says floating-point data in Dst "is handled with full precision (FP32 / BF16 / FP16) regardless of fidelity phase" (SrcASrcB.md:117). That is true, but "full precision" for a BF16 Dst is only 8 bits, so late phases may have nothing left to improve.

On NVIDIA tensor cores, BF16 inputs are typically accumulated in FP32, so the accumulator is rarely the weak link. On Tenstorrent the default Dst format for BF16 work is BF16 (fp32_dest_acc_en = false in the compute config, matrix_engine.md:53-58), and you opt into FP32 at the cost of half of Dst. Fidelity phases also have a GPU cousin: the "3xTF32" trick splits each FP32 number into a big and a small TF32 part and runs three tensor-core products (big·big, big·small, small·big), skipping small·small, to recover FP32 accuracy (Ootomo & Yokota, linked below). Tensix does the same split in hardware, and lets you choose how many pieces to pay for.

Measuring fidelity in the simulator

ttsim is bit-exact, so it is the right tool for measuring error. It is not a timing model, so it is the wrong tool for measuring speed. The workbench's mm_fid program is tt-metal's single-core matmul example with two knobs added: the fidelity and the random seed. It multiplies a 640×640 by a 640×640 BF16 matrix (inputs uniform in [0, 1)) on one core, compares the result with a CPU golden matmul that accumulates in FP32 and rounds once to BF16, and prints PCC, mean and max absolute error, and a hash of the result bits.

tenstorrent-from-zero/sim-labs/workbench/host/mm_fid.cpp:106-108
const char* fid_env = std::getenv("MM_FIDELITY");
tt::tt_metal::MathFidelity math_fidelity =
    fid_env ? static_cast<tt::tt_metal::MathFidelity>(std::atoi(fid_env)) : tt::tt_metal::MathFidelity::HiFi4;
MM_FIDELITY is parsed with atoi, so pass the enum number: 0, 2, 3 or 4. Passing MM_FIDELITY=HiFi2 parses as 0, and you would silently get LoFi.

What the numbers say:

  • HiFi3 and HiFi4 produce identical bits with a BF16 Dst (same hash, ef7abcd1…). This is the calculator's BF16 observation at full scale. Phase 3's terms are at most 2⁻¹¹ of a product, and with all-positive inputs the running Dst value is at least as large as each product, so phase 3 is always below half a BF16 ulp and rounds to zero.
  • With a BF16 Dst, accumulation is the dominant error. Outputs are sums of 640 products, about 160, where one BF16 ulp is 1.0. A mean error of about 1.5 is roughly 1.5 ulps, whatever the fidelity. Only LoFi stands out (2.2).
  • With an FP32 Dst, fidelity is the dominant error, and it behaves as the calculator predicts. LoFi truncates three bits of SrcA and one of SrcB, always downward. For uniform data that is a bias of roughly 2%, and 2% of ~160 ≈ 3.4, against a measured 3.32. HiFi2 leaves out only SrcB's last bit, about 0.25%, so 0.25% of 160 ≈ 0.4 (measured 0.405). HiFi3 and HiFi4 are within one BF16 ulp of the golden almost everywhere. Notice, though, that HiFi4 (0.0110) comes out worse than HiFi3 (0.0034). Each product is exact at HiFi4, but the accumulation is not. When mvmul aligns each group of 8 products to their largest exponent, it rounds ties upward (tensix.cpp:1729). With all-positive data this leaves the FP32 Dst slightly above the exact sum. HiFi3 leaves out phase 3, which only ever makes products smaller, and that cancels part of the upward bias. I checked this outside tt-metal by compiling ttsim's own mvmul into a small driver and running 20,000 random 640-term dot products in the LLK's phase order. The driver reproduced the effect: the mean offset from the exact sum was +0.011 at HiFi4 and +0.0035 at HiFi3, and the error against the golden after BF16 rounding was 0.0109 and 0.0030. Changing just that one rounding step to round-half-even made HiFi4 the most accurate (0.0012, versus 0.0064 for HiFi3). So this ordering is a quirk of the rounding that ttsim models, not a sign that phase 3 hurts. I have not confirmed it on silicon. Because the rounding is applied to magnitudes (ties away from zero), I would expect the bias to largely cancel for mixed-sign data, but I have not tested that.
  • PCC hides bias. FP32-Dst LoFi has a larger mean error (3.32) than BF16-Dst LoFi (2.22) but a higher PCC (0.9966 vs 0.9809). PCC measures correlation, and a nearly uniform 2% shrink is perfectly correlated with the truth. Check absolute or relative error too, not just PCC (see A05).
Go deeper in B07, Matmul and math fidelity, bit for bit: run mm_fid at each fidelity, dump one MVMUL's SrcA, SrcB and Dst from an instrumented ttsim, and reproduce the Dst bits in Python. The calculator's code above is a ready-made reference for that last step.

Deriving peak TFLOPS

Now you can derive a chip's headline number yourself. Each Tensix core's FPU retires at most one MVMUL per cycle, and each MVMUL is 4096 FLOP. With p fidelity phases, each useful MVMUL takes p instructions. So:

peak FLOP/s = 4096 × cores × clock ÷ phases

The ISA states the per-core figures for Wormhole at 1 GHz (4.096 / 2.048 / 1.366 / 1.024 TFLOP/s for 1–4 phases) and says to multiply by the number of Tensix tiles in the product: 72 for n150, 2 × 64 for n300, 80 per chip in Galaxy (MatrixUnit.md:62-77).   • FP8 and BFP4/BFP2 need 1 phase. BLOCKFP8 is quoted at 2 phases for Wormhole (148 for n150, the ISA's "full precision" count) but at 1 phase for the Blackhole cards (664). The same label hides a 2× difference in assumptions.
  • 16-bit: the Wormhole cards quote FP16 at 4 phases (74), but Hot Chips quotes Blackhole BF16 at 2 phases (373 = 745 / 2), the ISA's "reasonable" setting. The marketed "BF16 TFLOPS" is a choice of fidelity, not a property of the silicon.
  • TF32 at 4 phases (186 = 745 / 4), consistent with the ISA. TF32 can never be exact in SrcA.
Two unexplained discrepancies. (1) The Wormhole cards' FP8 figures are about 0.89× the formula, for both n150 (262 vs 294.9) and n300 (466 vs 524.3). Both equal 4.096 × 8/9 = 3.641 TF per core: 72 × 3.641 = 262.1 and 128 × 3.641 = 466.0. Yet BLOCKFP8 and FP16 on the same page match the formula exactly. The Galaxy page's 292 per 80-core module shows the same factor (292 / 327.7 = 0.891). So the factor does not come from core count: n150's 262 happens to equal 64 × 4.096 exactly, but no whole number of cores fits 466 on n300 (113.8) or 292 on Galaxy (71.3). I found no source that explains the 8/9 factor. It might be a lower clock assumed for FP8 (about 0.89 GHz), but that is a guess. Treat 262 as published and 295 as the ISA-derived ceiling. (2) Every Blackhole Hot Chips figure is 0.962× the formula at 1.35 GHz. Solving 745 = 140 × 4096 × f gives f ≈ 1.30 GHz, so the slides probably assumed a 1.3 GHz clock (inferred; the slides state no clock). Two caveats keep this an upper bound:

  • Short matrices waste the engine. An MVMUL always processes 8 rows of SrcB. If the left matrix has only 1 row, as in LLM decode with batch 1, 7/8 of the work is wasted: "the effective throughput will be 1/8" (GEMM_FLOPS.md:60-61). Tick the checkbox to see it. Chapter A22 shows why decode is limited by DRAM anyway.
  • Peak is not achieved. The GEMM tech report measured about 190 TFLOP/s on Wormhole and 580 on Blackhole (GEMM_FLOPS.md:86), using a compute grid of 8×8 cores on Wormhole and 13×10 on Blackhole (GEMM_FLOPS.md:108). It defines utilisation as ideal cycles (using 16/32/48/64 cycles per tile) over actual cycles, with peaks of about 93% (WH) and 96% (BH) (GEMM_FLOPS.md:104-113). Getting there takes the blocking and data reuse of A15 and the performance thinking of A20.

Recap

  • The FPU works only on registers. SrcA and SrcB each hold 2 banks × 64 rows × 16 × 19-bit, one tile per bank, double-buffered between unpacker and FPU. Dst is 1024 × 16 × 16-bit: 16 BF16 tiles or 8 FP32 tiles, halved under half-sync.
  • MVMUL computes Dst(8×16) += SrcB(8×16) @ SrcA(16×16): 2048 MACs = 4096 FLOP, one per cycle. The matmul's in0 goes to SrcB and in1 to SrcA.
  • A 32×32×32 tile product is 2×2×2 face products × 2 slabs = 16 MVMULs, recorded once in the replay buffer and replayed once per fidelity phase: 16/32/48/64 cycles.
  • The multipliers are 5-bit (SrcA) × 7-bit (SrcB). A BF16 significand (8 bits) is split 5|3 on SrcA and 7|1 on SrcB, giving four pieces (35 + 21 + 5 + 3 = 64 partial products). Phase p uses SrcA's low piece if bit 0 of p is set and SrcB's low piece if bit 1 is set, with the exponent lowered by 5 and 7 to match.
  • LoFi = phase 0, HiFi2 = 0–1, HiFi3 = 0–2, HiFi4 = all. FP8/BFP4/BFP2 are exact in 1 phase, BFP8 in 2, BF16 in 4; TF32/FP16 are never exact in SrcA.
  • Dst accumulates at its own precision. With a BF16 Dst, late phases are often rounded away: in ttsim, HiFi3 and HiFi4 gave identical bits on a 640³ matmul, while with an FP32 Dst the error fell from 3.32 (LoFi) to 0.0034 (HiFi3). HiFi4's slightly larger 0.011 comes from the way ttsim rounds the accumulation.
  • Peak = 4096 × cores × clock ÷ phases. It matches every published Tenstorrent figure except Wormhole's FP8 (×8/9, unexplained) and Blackhole's Hot Chips numbers (consistent with 1.3 GHz). ttsim's clock counts say nothing about speed.

Check yourself

How many MVMUL instructions does one 32×32 by 32×32 tile product issue at HiFi2, and what is its best-case FPU time on a 1 GHz Wormhole?

32 instructions and 32 ns. The tile needs 16 MVMULs: 8 face products of 16×16 @ 16×16, each split into two 8-row slabs. The LLK replays that 16-instruction sequence once per phase, and HiFi2 has 2 phases. At one MVMUL per cycle that is 32 cycles, or 32 ns at 1 GHz. LoFi would be 16 and HiFi4 64.

Why does BFP8 reach full precision with phases 0 and 1, while BF16 needs all four?

A BFP8_b value has 7 mantissa bits and stores its leading 1 explicitly among them, so it has at most 7 significant bits. Those always fit SrcB's 7-bit slice, so the SrcB-low phases (2 and 3) multiply only zeros, while SrcA still needs both its 5-bit and 3-bit slices (phases 0 and 1). BF16 has a full 8 significant bits, so SrcB's lowest bit also needs its own phases.

A colleague runs a BF16 matmul with the default Dst format and finds HiFi3 and HiFi4 give bit-identical results. Bug?

Not necessarily. Phase 3 multiplies SrcA's low 3 bits by SrcB's lowest bit, worth at most 7/16384 ≈ 2⁻¹¹ of a product. ttsim rounds each term to Dst's grid as it aligns it (tensix.cpp:1505-1510). With a BF16 Dst and same-sign data, that term is always below half an ulp of the running sum, so it rounds to zero. The mm_fid run above shows exactly this (same hash). With fp32_dest_acc_en the two would differ.

What is the peak HiFi4 TFLOP/s of an n150, and what does it become for a decode matmul whose left matrix has a single row?

4096 × 72 × 1 GHz ÷ 4 ≈ 73.7 TFLOP/s, which matches the published FP16 figure of 74. With one row, each MVMUL still processes 8 rows of SrcB but only one is useful, so the effective peak is 1/8 of that, about 9.2 TFLOP/s. In practice decode is limited by DRAM bandwidth long before that.

In ttsim's mvmul, phase 2 sets man_b = (man_b & 15) << 3 and lowers the exponent by 7. Why 7 and not 4?

SrcB's significand is split at bit 4: the high piece is man_b >> 4 and the low piece is man_b & 15, so the low piece is worth 2⁻⁴ relative to the high piece's unit. But it is also shifted left by 3 to fill the 7-bit multiplier input, which multiplies it by 2³. Undoing both takes 4 + 3 = 7. For SrcA the low piece (man_a >> 1) & 31 versus man_a >> 6 differs by 2⁵, hence −5.

The FP32-Dst LoFi run had PCC 0.9966 but the BF16-Dst LoFi run only 0.9809, yet the first has the larger mean error. How can both be true?

PCC measures how well the outputs correlate with the golden, not how close they are. LoFi truncation shrinks every product by roughly the same ~2%, so with an FP32 Dst the result is close to a scaled copy of the truth: large absolute error, but very high correlation. With a BF16 Dst, rounding after every MVMUL adds noise that does not correlate with the truth, which lowers PCC. Two error sources are mixed there, so mean error and PCC need not move together. Always look at absolute or relative error as well as PCC.
